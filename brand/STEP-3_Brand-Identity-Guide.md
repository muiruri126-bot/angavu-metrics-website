## 2. Brand Voice and Tone

### Voice (Consistent Across All Communications)

The Angavu Metrics voice is **confident, clear, evidence-based, and respectful.** We speak with authority earned through rigour, not asserted through jargon. We are direct without being blunt, technical without being inaccessible, and warm without being casual.

**Key voice principles:**
- **Say less, mean more.** Every sentence should carry weight.
- **Lead with the insight, not the method.** Audiences care about what the evidence shows before they care how we found it.
- **Respect the reader's intelligence.** Avoid over-explaining, but never assume shared knowledge of technical frameworks.
- **Use "we" language.** Position Angavu as a collaborative partner, not a detached expert.

### Tone Variations by Context

#### Proposals and RFP Responses
- **Tone:** Confident, precise, solution-oriented.
- Use active voice. State capabilities clearly and directly.
- Demonstrate understanding of the client's challenge before presenting our approach.
- Avoid generic claims. Anchor every capability statement in methodology or evidence.
- *Example:* "We propose a mixed-methods baseline study combining household surveys (n=1,200) with participatory focus groups across eight programme counties. This design enables both statistical rigour and contextual depth."

#### Donor and Government Presentations
- **Tone:** Authoritative, structured, insight-led.
- Lead with findings and implications, not process.
- Use data visualisations and evidence snapshots to anchor key points.
- Be concise — respect the time constraints of senior decision-makers.
- *Example:* "Three of the five outcome indicators show statistically significant improvement. The two lagging areas — institutional capacity and cross-county coordination — require targeted adaptive measures in Year 3."

#### Learning Briefs and Reports
- **Tone:** Reflective, honest, constructive.
- Balance rigour with readability.
- Highlight what worked, what did not, and what the evidence suggests for adaptation.
- Use clear section headings, executive summaries, and visual highlights.
- *Example:* "The programme's community feedback mechanism reached 68% of targeted households — above the 60% threshold — but qualitative data suggests response quality declines significantly in hard-to-reach sub-counties."

#### Website and General Communication
- **Tone:** Approachable, confident, clear.
- Slightly less formal than proposals, but never casual.
- Use shorter sentences and more direct language.
- Emphasise impact and value over technical process.
- *Example:* "We help organisations turn data into decisions. From programme design to impact evaluation, Angavu Metrics delivers the clarity needed to demonstrate results and adapt with confidence."

## 3. Brand Positioning Statement

Angavu Metrics is a specialist consultancy at the intersection of monitoring and evaluation, impact research, and policy advisory. We serve NGOs, development partners, and government institutions that need rigorous, context-sensitive evidence to design better programmes, demonstrate results, and strengthen systems.

In an ecosystem where many firms offer technical evaluation services, Angavu Metrics stands apart through three commitments: **contextual depth** — we understand the institutional, political, and community dynamics that shape programme outcomes in Africa and beyond; **methodological integrity** — we apply mixed-methods research, theory of change frameworks, OECD-DAC criteria, and systems thinking with discipline and transparency; and **practical utility** — our deliverables are designed to be used, not shelved. Every evaluation, every MEAL system, and every policy brief is built to inform real decisions by real people.

Headquartered in Kenya and working globally, Angavu Metrics bridges the perspectives of donors seeking accountability, governments pursuing reform, and communities whose lives programmes are designed to improve. We bring the clarity — *angavu* — needed to see what the data reveals and to act on it with confidence.

## 4. Visual Style Guidance

### Reports and Policy Briefs

- **Layout:** Clean, grid-based layouts with generous white space. Use clear heading hierarchies (H1 for title, H2 for sections, H3 for subsections).
- **Colour usage:** Restrained. Primary blue for headings and section dividers. Teal or accent colour for callout boxes, key findings, and data highlights. Body text in charcoal.
- **Data presentation:** Use simple, clean charts (bar, line, donut). Avoid 3D effects, unnecessary gridlines, or decorative elements. Label directly on charts where possible.
- **Key findings:** Use a coloured callout box or left-border highlight (teal or gold) to draw attention to headline insights.
- **Photography:** If used, prefer authentic fieldwork imagery — community meetings, government workshops, data collection in the field — over stock photos.

### Dashboards and Data Visualisations

- **Colour system:** Use the primary palette consistently. Blue for primary metrics, teal for secondary, gold for highlights or alerts, grays for context.
- **Typography:** Use the heading font for dashboard titles and metric labels. Keep font sizes functionally large and legible.
- **Layout:** Card-based design with clear sections. Each card should answer one question. Avoid dashboard clutter.
- **Interactions:** Progressive disclosure — summary first, detail on demand.

### Slide Decks and Presentations

- **Template structure:** Title slide → Agenda → Content slides → Summary / Recommendations → Contact.
- **Content density:** One idea per slide. Use short statements, not paragraphs.
- **Visual emphasis:** Use a bold accent colour (teal or gold) for the single most important number or finding on each slide.
- **Backgrounds:** White or warm white. Avoid dark backgrounds unless for a specific visual impact slide.

### Design Principles

1. **Lead with clarity.** Every design decision should make information easier to find, read, and understand. If a visual element does not aid comprehension, remove it.

2. **Highlight key insights visually.** Use colour, size, and position to direct the reader's eye to what matters most. Executive summaries, key findings, and recommendations should be impossible to miss.

3. **Use consistent layout grids.** Establish and maintain consistent margins, column widths, and spacing across all materials. Consistency builds trust.

4. **Anchor in evidence.** Every visual — whether a chart, icon, or infographic — should be tied to a data point, finding, or framework. Decorative-only elements dilute credibility.

5. **Design for decision-makers.** Assume the reader has limited time and is looking for actionable information. Structure every document so it can be skimmed in 60 seconds and read in detail when needed.
